Wind model variability

The sections above draw the latest run. This draws the last 6 HRRR runs against each other at the same water point, which answers a different question: not what the wind will be, but how much the forecast moves when the model is re-run. Six runs agreeing on 8 kt this afternoon and six runs scattered between 3 and 14 look identical in a single forecast. REFS, NOAA's ensemble, answers the same question the designed way — many members started differently on purpose — and its spread sits beside the HRRR runs' so the two can be compared. Read wide spread as a warning and narrow spread as weak reassurance, not as a probability: successive runs of one model share its biases, so a systematic error looks like perfect agreement.

Under the Navigation Rules (Rule 20) running lights are shown from sunset to sunrise, and in restricted visibility by day — so sunset, not dark, is when they go on. Instability — the ingredient underneath

Two fields, always together. CAPE is how much energy a rising parcel of air would gain; CIN is the cap it has to break through to get at it. Either alone misleads in the same direction — a capped 900 J/kg afternoon and an uncapped one are completely different days and read identically if only the energy is shown. Both are the most within the same 25 nm ring as the lightning above, so "unstable here" means the same here; the ring's east edge is the Cascade foothills, where an afternoon heats far more CAPE than the water does, so the readout says where the most sits. This does not set the lightning level. Instability is necessary for convection and nowhere near sufficient, and most hours with real CAPE on them pass without a thing happening.

Why the cap is not simply "worse" — and every hour's numbers

The cap boundaries are Stull's (Practical Meteorology, Table 14-4) rather than anything invented here, and the CAPE ranges are the conventional operational ones. More cap is not simply worse, which is the part worth knowing. Under 20 J/kg the energy releases as it arrives, in scattered weak convection that never accumulates. Between 20 and 60 is the most favorable — Stull's own note is "best conditions" — because the lid holds long enough for real energy to build and still breaks when something lifts the air: a front, the convergence zone, the afternoon sea breeze meeting the land. Past 60 it needs an exceptionally strong trigger, and past 100 it usually wins.

That non-monotonic shape is why there is no single combined number here. An index would have to rise or fall consistently with each input, and this one does neither — and there is no standard CAPE-and-CIN scalar to borrow. The composite parameters that do fold CIN in are tuned for Plains supercells and would be nonsense over Puget Sound. Both values are the extreme anywhere in the ring for that hour — the most energy, the strongest cap — which need not be the same cell.

Puget Sound Convergence Zone

Flow off the Pacific splits around the Olympics and meets again over the Sound, pinned there by the Cascades. Where the two branches collide the air has nowhere to go but up, and a narrow east–west band of heavy showers sets up with quiet, sinking air on either side of it. It is the most locally distinctive thing the weather here does, and a point forecast for this water cannot see it: the band often sits a few miles north of the breakwater, which is sun at the marina and a squall line off Edmonds — with the wind southerly on its south side and northerly on its north.

There is no likelihood score here, deliberately. The obvious design is a number out of wind direction, speed and season. Mass (1981) is the foundational study and says the zone is skilfully forecast from surface coastal winds — but neither it nor what followed publishes cut points, and the descriptive sources are explicit that the zone is variable in both location and strength. Every weight in such a score would be this page's own invention wearing the clothes of a probability.
So it measures the thing instead. A convergence zone is low-level convergence, and HRRR gives the 10 m wind on a 3 km grid over the Sound, so −(∂u/∂x + ∂v/∂y) is a measurement rather than a stand-in. It also answers where, which a score never could.

Each column is one forecast hour; each row a strip of latitude across the Sound, from Tacoma at the bottom to north of Everett at the top, with Shilshole marked. Blue is convergence — air arriving with nowhere to go but up. A zone shows as a band: a run of blue with sinking air above and below it, which is why the chart is a profile and not a single number. Uniform light convergence over the whole Sound is not a convergence zone, and the two would look identical in a headline figure.
The wind is smoothed to about 27 km before the divergence is taken. Differentiating the raw 3 km field amplifies its noise — the version of this that did exactly that reported the Olympic foothills as the strongest convergence on the map.
HRRR reaches Sat 26 Sep 10:00 to Mon 28 Sep 05:00 PDT, each hour from the freshest run that covers it: the hourly run (15Z) first, then the older extended one (12Z), where the solid rule marks the step back. Nothing past Mon 28 Sep 05:00 is measured here.

Exactly how the number is calculated

HRRR gives two wind components on its native 3 km grid: u and v, in metres per second, along the grid's own rows and columns — which over the Sound run about 16° anticlockwise of east and north. Divergence is how fast air is spreading out at a point: ∂u/∂x + ∂v/∂y, the rate u changes along a row plus the rate v changes up a column. Negate it and you have convergence: air arriving faster than it leaves, which over a surface has nowhere to go but up. Divergence comes out the same whichever way the axes point, so long as the components and the differences are taken along the same ones; this is done on the grid's axes and needs no rotation.
In practice, per forecast hour:

  1. u and v are each box-averaged over a 9 × 9 window of grid cells — about 27 km square on the ground.
  2. ∂u/∂x is a centred difference between the cells either side, divided by the distance between them measured off their coordinates (2.95 km here, not an assumed 3); ∂v/∂y is the same going up the column.
  3. The two are added and the sign flipped. Units are inverse seconds; the values are shown as ×10−5 s−1 because that is the order the atmosphere works at.
  4. Within each 0.1° strip of latitude — about 6 nm — the result is averaged across the Sound, from -122.75° to -122.05°. A zone is an east–west band, so averaging along it is signal; averaging across it would be what destroys the signal.

On resolution, honestly. A convergence-zone band is typically 10 to 40 km wide. The strips resolve that comfortably, but the 27 km smoothing is at the coarse end of it and will blur a narrow, sharp band into a broader, weaker one. That is a deliberate trade, made on the Lake Union page: at 15 km the band-to-band roughness of the profile was nearly double, and structure that moved 10 km in an hour was indistinguishable from noise. What this chart is good at is telling you a band exists and roughly where; it is not good at telling you the band is exactly 3 miles wide, or which side of the breakwater its edge will fall.

How the coastal flow is worked out, and what is this page's own choice in it

Same HRRR file, same 10 m wind. Every grid cell in an offshore box — -124.9° to -124.2° longitude, 46.9° to 48.1° latitude, out in the Pacific west of the Olympics — is turned from the grid's axes to true east and north, and the mean of them is given as the bearing the wind comes from. That box is upwind of the split, which is where Mass says the useful predictor lives. Its speed is the mean of the cells' own speeds, in knots, not the length of the mean vector, which would call a box of strong disagreeing wind calm.
Three honest caveats.

  1. “West through north-west” is taken here as 260–340°, and those two numbers are this page's, not the literature's. Mass describes the regime in words and publishes no bearings. The count above is reported beside the measurement rather than multiplied into it, which keeps the stakes low — but it is still a cut point, and an unlabelled cut point is exactly what this section refuses elsewhere.
  2. The box only gets to answer when it agrees with itself. Averaging hundreds of cells produces a direction whether or not they point the same way. On the Lake Union page's first hour tested the per-cell bearings ran from 10° to 323° with a circular R of 0.17 — the mean of a nearly random set — and the first version reported “from 60°” as calmly as it would a uniform gale. Hours below R 0.6 or under 4 kt are counted as no coherent flow rather than as “not in the regime”, because those are different statements and only one of them is evidence.
  3. HRRR's winds are relative to its grid, not to north. Over the offshore box the grid is turned about 17°, so an unrotated bearing would be 17° veered — enough to move an hour across either edge of the regime. The bearing here is rotated first.

Radar & satellite

Simulated reflectivity — the next 18 hours

The radar above is where weather is. This is where HRRR thinks it will be — the model's own composite reflectivity, hour by hour, over a 500 km frame centred on the water off Shilshole (the dot). It answers a different question from the radar: not whether it is raining now, but whether a band will have crossed before you are back on the dock. Play runs it round until paused, and pausing returns to the hour nearest your own clock; step it with the arrows or drag across the frame.

This is a model, not an observation. It uses the same color scale as real radar, which makes it easy to read as a measurement. Treat the timing and the shape as the useful part, and the exact placement of any one cell as approximate.
Why it stops at 18 hours, and why this frame

It stops at the hourly run's own length, and HRRR's 48-hour run is deliberately not offered here. Reflectivity skill decays much faster than the model's other fields; past half a day HRRR is putting convection somewhere plausible rather than where it will be, and a radar-shaped picture at that range would claim more than it can support. The point forecast above answers the two-day question as a chance of rain at this water — the form a forecast can actually defend.

Below 5 dBZ is left blank: there the model is drawing cloud and numerical noise, and painting it would fill the frame with a haze that reads as weather. The colours are banded rather than a smooth ramp for the same reason — a ramp invites telling 41 dBZ from 44, a precision the model does not have at 18 hours. Each 3 km model cell is drawn as a square block, unsmoothed, and the frame is the model's own grid with the coastline bent to match it rather than the data resampled to fit a map.
